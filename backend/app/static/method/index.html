How it works

How the model was built, trained and tested

The goal was the most trustworthy answer, not the most impressive score. Each step below links to its evidence.

  1. 01

    Start from a clear question

    To what extent can patient characteristics available near admission predict length of stay, and how reliably can models quantify uncertainty? A second question: do nonlinear models beat simple baselines by a statistically meaningful margin?

  2. 02

    Use real data, and say what it is

    The app uses the UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals dataset: 101,766 real, de-identified inpatient stays (1999–2008) of adults with diabetes, 1–14 days long. Only each patient's first stay is kept (repeat stays removed), so the same person never appears in both training and test data. Stays ending in death or hospice are excluded, because that is not a discharge. The project first ran on a synthetic Kaggle dataset, where nothing was predictable. That run is kept for comparison.

    Synthetic vs real
  3. 03

    Block data leakage

    The admission-time model sees only what is plausibly known on arrival: age band, gender, admission type and source, primary diagnosis group, diabetes medication regimen, HbA1c and glucose ranges, and visits in the previous year (feature set A). Counts accumulated during the stay (lab tests, procedures, medications, diagnoses), the medication-change flag and the discharge destination would leak the answer. They live only in research set B, which shows how much they would inflate accuracy. Race is never a predictor; it is used only to check that errors are fair.

    Set A vs set B
  4. 04

    Lock away a final test set

    20% of patients were set aside before any modelling. They played no part in preprocessing, feature selection, tuning, early stopping or model choice, and are scored once at the very end. A further 15% of the development data is reserved only for calibrating prediction intervals.

  5. 05

    Train seven models, from naive to neural

    Two naive baselines (overall median; median by condition), a Gamma GLM with log link as the interpretable statistical baseline, XGBoost (target transform chosen during tuning), an XGBoost survival (AFT) model, CatBoost for the categorical features, and an FT-Transformer in PyTorch with feature embeddings, multi-head attention, residual connections, layer norm, dropout, weight decay, a warm-up/cosine learning-rate schedule, gradient clipping, early stopping and best-checkpoint restoration. Every model fits its own preprocessing inside each training fold.

  6. 06

    Tune inside nested cross-validation

    Outer 5-fold CV estimates performance. Inside each outer training portion, an inner 5-fold CV (3 for the transformer) runs the Optuna search, choosing hyper-parameters, the feature subset and preprocessing. The outer fold never informs tuning. Early stopping uses a slice of the training rows, never the evaluation fold. Every trial is stored with its seed.

    Fold results
  7. 07

    Repeat, resample, and stress-test

    The final configurations are re-scored with 3× repeated 5-fold CV on identical folds for every model. Test predictions are bootstrapped 1,000 times for BCa confidence intervals, and paired bootstrap differences show whether one model really beats another. As a negative control, the target is shuffled and the models are retrained to give an empirical permutation p-value. Learning curves check for over- and under-fitting, and LOOCV is demonstrated on a subsample only.

    Negative control
  8. 08

    Choose by evidence, not by the smallest number

    Models are ranked by nested-CV MAE and compared with the leader using a corrected resampled t-test, which allows for overlapping folds. The simplest model that is statistically indistinguishable from the leader wins. The choice is written to disk before the test set is opened.

    Selection & verdict
  9. 09

    Quantify uncertainty for each patient

    Split-conformal prediction turns each estimate into an interval with guaranteed average coverage. The test set checks coverage overall and by subgroup, and the width is compared with a no-information interval. Remaining stay = predicted total − days already spent, clamped at zero. A survival-adjusted version conditions on the patient still being in hospital.

    Interval coverage
  10. 10

    Explain without over-claiming

    TreeSHAP shows how CatBoost and XGBoost moved each prediction. GLM coefficients come with standard errors, confidence intervals, AIC, AICc and BIC, and information criteria are used only among likelihood-based models. The transformer gets permutation importance; attention weights are not treated as explanations. None of these are causal.

    Feature importance
  11. 11

    Report what was found

    A quality gate checks five failure modes: no gain over the median baseline, models indistinguishable from one another, shuffled-target parity, intervals as wide as a no-information interval, and poor calibration. Any failure puts a warning on every prediction. Finding that a dataset holds little signal, and showing it rigorously, is a valid result.

Limitations

The data are real but narrow: US hospitals, 1999–2008, adults with diabetes, stays capped at 14 days, and age known only in 10-year bands. Many important drivers of length of stay, such as vital signs, severity scores and social circumstances, are not in the data, so admission-time predictions remain uncertain. Associations are not causes. Nothing here is a clinical discharge decision.

Educational research prototype trained on de-identified records of US inpatients with diabetes (UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals, 1999–2008). Not medical advice, and never a clinical discharge decision.
